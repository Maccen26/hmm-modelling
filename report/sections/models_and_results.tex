\section{Models \& Results}
For generel theory on HMM's, see \citep{zucchini2016hidden} and \citep{moler2024statistical} chapter 11. 
All the models implemented are 4 state Gaussian HMMs (emission density is a Gaussian distribution) 
and we have kept the the mean value of state 1 fixed at 400 as this is the baseline $\text{CO}_2$ level in the room 
when no one is present and all windows are closed. This is also shown in the figure \ref{fig:app:co2_data}. All models werre
fitted with the L-BFGS optimizer and the same initialisation strategy was used for all models, which is described in the results section of each model.

Five models are considered, organised along two axes: the order of the AR
component in the emission and the order of the latent Markov chain. Their
relationship is shown in Figure \ref{fig:diagram:model_hierarchy}.
\input{sections/diagrams/fig_06_model_hierarchy}
For each level in the hierarchy tree in \ref{fig:diagram:model_hierarchy} 
starting from HMM(1), a method for capturing the temporal 
structure of the data is added. 
The HMM(1) is the ordinary HMM, 
which does not capture any temporal structure in 
the data.

\subsection{Ordinary HMM model}
\subsubsection{Theory}
The ordinary HMM model is the simplest model implemented in this project and the one most commonly used in the literature.
The transition probabilities are constant and do not depend on time, the observations or any covariates, and so does the emission density.
\newline
For this model, we want to estimate the parameters $\theta = \{\Gamma, \mu, \sigma\}$ 
where $\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the transition matrix, $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^n$ 
is the mean vector of the Gaussian emission density and $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^n$ 
is the variance vector of the Gaussian emission density.
Because the markov chain is finite, discrete and homogeneous, 
a unique strictly positive stationary distribution $\delta$ exists 
(chappter 1.3.2 \citep{zucchini2016hidden}) and is given by
\begin{equation}
    \delta=\mathbf{1}^T(I-\Gamma+E)^{-1}
    \label{eq:stationary_dist}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is a matrix of ones. \newline

To handle the natural bounds on the standard deviation parameters and transition proberbilities, the parameters
were reparameterized as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i = \exp(\eta_i)
    \label{eq:sigma_reparam}
\end{equation}
For the transition matrix,the $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal entries are
parametrized. The diagonal is used as the reference category with
log-odds fixed to zero such that a transtion matrix on the from
\begin{equation}
    \xi = \begin{bmatrix}
        - & \xi_{12} & \cdots & \xi_{1n} \\
        \xi_{21} & - & \cdots & \xi_{2n} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \xi_{n1} & \xi_{n2} & \cdots & -  
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Concretely, the unconstrained parameters are
stored as a matrix $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n-1)}$ that holds the
off-diagonal logits. The transition matrix is then obtained by
exponentiating these logits in the off-diagonal positions of an
identity matrix (so the diagonal is $\exp(0) = 1$) and row-normalizing:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{ij} =
    \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{\exp(\xi_{ij})}{1 + \sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\xi_{ik})}, & j \neq i, \\[6pt]
    \dfrac{1}{1 + \sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\xi_{ik})}, & j = i.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:gamma_softmax}
\end{equation}

The unconstrained parameters $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and
$\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n-1)}$ are the quantities that are
optimized during the fitting process. \newline

The $\mu$ parameters are not bounded, but to avoid label switching for 
the gaussitan distrbitions, the mean parameters are ordered as $\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \mu_3 < \mu_4$ by the reparameterization
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mu_1 &= \zeta_1 \\
        \mu_i &= \zeta_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{i} \exp(\zeta_k)
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mu_reparam}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the unconstrained parameter vector that is optimized during the fitting process. \newline

The model was fitted on the $\text{CO}_2$ observations by minimising the negative
log-likelihood with L-BFGS, with $\mu_1$ frozen at 400 ppm as discussed above.

and the estimated emission parameters in Table \ref{tab:ordinary_hmm_emissions}.

We see that for each state, there is a least $0.8$ probability of switching

The four estimated state means in Table \ref{tab:ordinary_hmm_emissions}

    \item state 2 and 3 are intermediate states where the occupants has entered the room

Figure \ref{fig:ordinary_hmm_diagnostics} summarises the fit. The
log-likelihood trace (left panel) shows that the optimiser converges quickly

\subsection{AR(1)-HMM}
\subsubsection{Theory}
The Autoregressive HMM (AR(1)-HMM) extends the ordinary HMM by letting the
emission distribution depend on the previous observation. Conditional on
being in state $i$ at time $t$, the observation is modelled as
\begin{equation}
    Y_t \mid X_t = i,\; Y_{t-1} = y_{t-1} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_i + \phi_i(y_{t-1} - \mu_i),\; \sigma_i^2\right),
    \label{eq:ar1_emission}
\end{equation}
Therefore, each states has its own AR(1) coefficient $\phi_i \in (-1, 1)$ in
addition to a mean and variance. This may be enough to capture the temporal
correlation of the CO$_2$ signal that the ordinary HMM left in the
pseudo-residuals. The transition matrix $\Gamma$ is parametrised exactly as in
the ordinary HMM, and the AR coefficients are reparametrised as
$\phi_i = 2\,\sigma(\psi_i) - 1$, where
$\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ is the logistic sigmoid, to enforce the
stationarity constraint $|\phi_i| < 1$. \newline
The unconstrained parameter
$\psi_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the one passed to the optimiser, with inverse
mapping $\psi_i = \operatorname{logit}\!\left(\tfrac{\phi_i + 1}{2}\right)$.

\subsubsection{Results} 
The ordinary-HMM estimates were used as a warm start for $\Gamma$, $\mu$ and
$\sigma$, and the AR coefficients were initialised at $\phi_i = 0.2$ for all
states. The estimated transition matrix is shown in
Table \ref{tab:ar_hmm_transition} and the estimated emission parameters in
Table \ref{tab:ar_hmm_emissions}.

\input{model_results/ar_hmm/transition_matrix}

The transition matrix stronngly dominant in the diagonal, 
with probabilities between $0.74$ and $0.97$. The off-diagonal pattern is
similar to the ordinary HMM, but now only 1 transition is impossible, which is 
state 3 to state 4. We switched self transition probabilites between state 2 and 3, meaning
AR(1)-HMM says it's more likely to stay in state 2 than state 3, while the ordinary HMM says the opposite.

\input{model_results/ar_hmm/emission_params}

The estimated state means in Table \ref{tab:ar_hmm_emissions} are similar to the ordinary HMM, 
with a clear ordering from low to high. We see that state 4 mean is close the HMM state 4 mean, 
but state 2 and 3 means are closer together than in the ordinary HMM. 
This validates the interpretation of these two states being transitional states 
between the baseline and the high-CO$_2$ state. 

All four AR coefficients are
positive and substantial, ranging from $\hat{\phi}_2 = 0.37$ up to
$\hat{\phi}_3 = 0.95$; the high-CO$_2$ state (state 4) also has a strong AR
component ($\hat{\phi}_4 = 0.83$). This confirms that a non-trivial amount of
the temporal structure in the observations is captured by the AR(1) component of the emission density. 
The positive AR coefficients also make sense as we would expect the CO$_2$ 
signal at a timestep $t$ to be positively 
correlated with the CO$_2$ ppm at time $t-1$ by logical thinking. 
\input{model_results/ar_hmm/diagnostics}

Figure \ref{fig:ar_hmm_diagnostics} shows the diagnostics for the AR-HMM. 
The log-likelihood trace (left panel) shows that the optimiser converges after a few
more iterations than for the ordinary HMM, which is expected as the AR-HMM has more parameters. 
The Q-Q plot of the pseudo-residuals (centre) shows a clear improvement in the 
normality of the residuals compared to the ordinary HMM, with only 
a single outlier in the lower tail and few more in the upper tail. 
The ACF however seems to have been worsend as there are more 
lags with significant residual spikes, 
and the first lags drops quickly to around 0.3 and then decays slowly.
We also see that after lag 15, almost all of the lags are negative. 
This indicates that while the AR(1) component captures a lot of the 
temporal structure in the data, there is still some autocorrelation left in the residuals. 
This motivates the introduction of the AR(2)-HMM in the next section, 
which adds a second-order autoregressive component to the emission density.
\subsection{AR(2)-HMM}
\subsubsection{Theory}
The Autoregressive of order 2 HMM (AR(2)-HMM) extends the AR-HMM 
by letting the autoregressive
component depend on the two most recent observations rather than just
one. Conditional on being in state $i$ at time $t$, the observation is
modelled as
\begin{equation}
    Y_t \mid X_t = i,\; y_{t-1}, y_{t-2} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_i + \phi_{i,1}(y_{t-1} - \mu_i) + \phi_{i,2}(y_{t-2} - \mu_i),\; \sigma_i^2\right),
    \label{eq:ar2_emission}
\end{equation}
so each state now has a pair of AR coefficients
$(\phi_{i,1}, \phi_{i,2})$ in addition to a mean and variance.

The AR-HMM estimates were used as a warm start, and the second-order AR coefficients were
initialised at $\phi_{i,2} = 0.2$ for all states. The estimated transition
matrix is shown in Table \ref{tab:ar_2_hmm_transition} and the estimated
emission parameters in Table \ref{tab:ar_2_hmm_emissions}.

\input{model_results/ar_2_hmm/transition_matrix}

The transition matrix is very close to the one obtained for the AR-HMM.
Adding a second lag therefore does not noticeably change the 
between-state dynamics of the model. 

\input{model_results/ar_2_hmm/emission_params}

The first-order AR coefficients in Table \ref{tab:ar_2_hmm_emissions} remain
strongly positive across all states ($0.59$--$1.00$), while the second-order
coefficients are small and mostly negative, ranging from $-0.21$ to $0.06$.
The sum of the AR coefficients resemble the AR(1) coefficients of the previous model, 
but the first lag is much higher and the second lag is negative, with except 
for state 3. This tells us that the obsevation at time $t$ 
is more positively correlated with the observation at time $t$ is 
highly effected by the observation at time $t-1$ and that the observation 
at time $t-2$ has a small negative effect on the observation at time $t$. 

\input{model_results/ar_2_hmm/diagnostics}

Figure \ref{fig:ar_2_hmm_diagnostics} shows the diagnostics for the
AR(2)-HMM. The log-likelihood converges more quickly to its optimum than AR(1)-HMM, 
even tough we have introduced an additional parameter, and the
Q-Q plot of the pseudo-residuals is similar to that of the AR(1)-HMM. 
The ACF plot however shows a clear improvement in the autocorrelation 
of the residuals, as fewer lags are outside the confidence band 
and the first lags are smalelr than in the AR(1)-HMM.
However, the patteren of the ACF is similar to the AR(1)-HMM and there

\subsection{AR(1)-HMM(2)}
\subsubsection{Theory}
The Autoregressive second-order HMM (AR(1)-HMM(2)) 
reformulates the Markov assumption on the latent chain. Now, the
next state depends on the two most recent latent states rather than
just one. The transition probabilities are therefore indexed by a pair of
states,
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{(i,j),k} \;=\; P\!\left(X_{t+1} = k \mid X_{t-1} = i,\, X_t = j\right),
    \label{eq:second_order_transition}
\end{equation}
which gives an $n^2 \times n$ tensor of transition probabilities (still
row-normalised in $k$). The emission density is again Gaussian, with the mean
depending only on the current state $\mu(x_t)$, but the standard deviation
allowed to depend on both the previous and the current state
$\sigma(x_{t-1}, x_t)$. When implementing the second-order chain, the 
state space is augmented to pairs $S_k = (x_{i, t-1}, x_{j, t})$ where $x_{t-k, i}$ is 
the state $i$ at time $t-k$. $S \in \mathbb{R}^M$ is recognized a the state distributuion. 
We can the define the possible transitinos in the augmented transition matrix $\Gamma^* \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M}$ 
as 
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma^*_{(i,j),(j,k)} = \Gamma^*_{(i,j),k}
    \label{eq:augmented_transition}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the next state given the current state $(i,j)$. 
This is implmented in the code by checking the that the current state $j$ 
is the previous state in the next state. 
We only store the $n^2 \times n$ log transition probability tensor $\xi$ and not the full $M \times M$ transition matrix $\Gamma^*$. 
When constructing the full transition matrix $\Gamma^*$, we fill the entries according to \ref{eq:augmented_transition} and set the rest of the entries to a
higly negative value. We then apply the row-wise softmax to get a valid transition matrix.

\subsubsection{Results}
The estimated transition tensor is shown in
Table \ref{tab:second_order_hmm_transition} and the estimated emission
parameters in Table \ref{tab:second_order_hmm_emissions}.

\input{model_results/second_order_hmm/transition_matrix}
We see in the estimated transition matrix that the previous state
you were in effect the transition probabilities of the next state.
For example, if you are in state 1 and you were in state 1 in the previous time step, 
then you have a high probability of staying in state 1 and a low probability of transitioning to state 4.
Where at, if you previously were in state 4 and is now in state 1, there is a much higher 
probability of transitioning to state 4. 
We also see that state 3 has low self-transition probabilities and 
the system in generel quicly transitions out of state 3, which is in line of state 3
being a transition state between the baseline and the high-CO$_2$ state. 
State 2 on the other hand has a higher self-transition probability, and 
its in generel more likely to transition to state 2 than state 3 given previous states. 
This indicates that state 2 is more of a "sticky" state than state 3. 

\input{model_results/second_order_hmm/emission_params.tex}

We see in Table \ref{tab:second_order_hmm_emissions} that the state means are similar to the previous models. 
The means are more evenly spread out and the high CO$_2$ state (state 4) has a higher mean than in the previous models. 
The standard deviations is about the same and almost all of the $|\phi|$ vals
are below 1. However, two are not. This is for the state pairs
$(3,4)$ and $(4,1)$. This could be explained by that when moving from
state 3 to state 4, the CO$_2$ level is rising and the mean of the next observation is higher than the current one, which could lead to a high positive AR coefficient. 
When moving from state 4 to state 1, the CO$_2$ level is 
dropping and one would expect a hight negative AR coefficient.
But looking at the transition matrix \ref{tab:second_order_hmm_transition}, we see
that its likely to transtion back into a higher CO$_2$ state after transitioning 
from state 4 to state 1, which could explain the high positive AR coefficient.

capturing the temporal structure in the data.

\subsection{AR(2)-HMM(2)}
\subsubsection{Theory}
The Autoregressive lag 2 Second-order HMM (AR(2)-HMM(2)) combines the two previous extensions. 
The markov chain is second-order (transitions depend on the 
pair $(x_{t-1}, x_t)$) and the emission density carries an AR(2) 
component whose parameters also depend
on the pair $(x_{t-1}, x_t)$.

The transition tensor follows the same overall pattern as the  AR(1)-HMM(2). 
rows like $(1,1)$, $(2,2)$ and $(4,4)$ keep most of their mass on the
current state. However, we see that for state 3 that self transtion

\input{model_results/ar_2_second_order_hmm/emission_params}

Table \ref{tab:ar_2_second_order_hmm_emissions} reports the pair-specific
emission parameters. The four state means are very close to the AR(1)-HMM(2)
estimates, with a clear ordering from the baseline state 1 ($400$ ppm) up to
the high-CO$_2$ state 4 ($1436$ ppm), and state 2 and 3 placed as
intermediate states. As before, the standard deviations grow with the mean,
which we attribute to more volatility in the CO$_2$ signal when occupants
are present and windows are being opened and closed. \newline

The first-order AR coefficients $\hat{\phi}_1$ remain strongly positive for
most state pairs (typically $0.7$--$1.0$), while the second-order
coefficients $\hat{\phi}_2$ are small and mostly negative, ranging from
$-0.27$ up to $0.17$. This mirrors the pattern seen for the AR(2)-HMM. 
The observation at time $t$ is dominated by the previous observation,
while $y_{t-2}$ contributes a small negative correction. The exception is
the pair $(1,2)$, where $\hat{\phi}_1 = -0.28$ and $\hat{\phi}_2 = 0.17$,
which corresponds to the system entering state 2 from the baseline and so
the level is increasing.

Several pairs have $\hat{\phi}_1 + \hat{\phi}_2 > 1$, indicating a
non-stationary AR process. These are the same kind of pairs that were
flagged for the AR(1)-HMM(2). Transitions where the CO$_2$ level is
changing sharply, such as $(3,4)$ and $(4,1)$, together with the
self-transitions $(3,3)$ and $(4,3)$. 

\input{model_results/ar_2_second_order_hmm/diagnostics}

Figure \ref{fig:ar_2_second_order_hmm_diagnostics} shows the diagnostics for
the AR(2)-HMM(2). The convergence of the log-likehood is not as smooth as 
previous models, but it still converges after a around 20 iterations. 
The Q-Q plot of the pseudo-residuals is similar to the previous models, with a few outliers in the tails. 
The ACF of the residuals shows a similar pattern as the previous models, with a slow decay and several lags outside the confidence band.
