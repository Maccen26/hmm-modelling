\section{Preprocessing of Data}
\subsection{Data Calibration}

When collecting $\text{CO}_2$ data, the sensors tend to drift over time \parencite{pulse2025co2calibration},
and therefore, the data collected from the sensors
are not always accurate. Two empty rooms that have the same $\text{CO}_2$ levels may show different
readings based on how much the sensors have drifted over time.
This is a problem, as we want to use the $\text{CO}_2$ data to infer the presence of the occupants in the building, and a
higher baseline of the $\text{CO}_2$ data may lead to a false positive of the presence of the occupants in the building.
%% --- Old calibration technique (ABC algorithm), kept for reference ---
% To correct for this, we have used a method called the
% \textit{Automatic Baseline Correction} (ABC) Algorithm [\textcolor{red}{MISSING REFERENCE}].
% In the use of this algorithm, we have assumed that the lowest $\text{CO}_2$ ppm values over a period

%% --- End of old calibration technique ---
We have assumed that the lowest $\text{CO}_2$ ppm values over a period
are equal to the outside $\text{CO}_2$ ppm values. We set the outside $\text{CO}_2$ to 420 ppm \parencite{icos2023ghg}.
\bigskip

Let $C_{t}$ be the $\text{CO}_2$ ppm value at time $t$, and $\tilde{C}_{t}$ be the corrected $\text{CO}_2$ ppm value at time $t$.

% \subsection{Causal anchor baseline}
% The rolling quantile in \cref{eq:calibration} uses the minimum of the whole series, and the

% causal baseline $b_t$, which only uses readings up to time $t$, such that a calibrated value is never revised.
%% --- End of old calibration technique ---
To correct for this, we use a causal anchor baseline $b_t$, which only uses readings up to time $t$,
such that a calibrated value is never revised.
The calibrated series is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:calibration_anchor}
\tilde{C}_{t} = C_{t} - b_{t} + 420.
\end{equation}
Let $\bar{C}_t$ be the trailing 2 hour mean of $C_t$, such that a single noisy reading cannot move the baseline.
The baseline starts at $b_0 = 420$ ppm and is updated by two rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule A (down fast):} If $\bar{C}_t < b_t$, the baseline is too high, as the room
    cannot be below fresh air. We then set $b_t = \bar{C}_t$ at once.
    \item \textbf{Rule B (up slowly):} For each day we find the minimum of $\bar{C}_t$. If the slope over the
    3 hours going into the minimum is below 3 ppm/h, the $\text{CO}_2$ has levelled off and the minimum is
    an \textit{anchor}. When the day is over, the baseline ramps linearly towards the anchor over 7 days,
    moving at most 30 ppm per anchor.
\end{itemize}
Finally, the data is resampled into 30 minute bins, and empty bins are forward-filled instead of
interpolated, since interpolation would use future readings.

\section{Summary Statistics }

As written in the introduction, our goal is to find and model a systematic behaviour of
the occupants and simulate a thermal comfort curve. To do this, we will only focus on office rooms, as
we hypothesise that they will be much more occupied and show a more regular
and systematic change in $\text{CO}_2$ ppm levels. For the second part,
 we will also need complaints from the users as to how the temperature is in the room.
\input{Tables/data/raw_co2_quantiles}
\Cref{tab:raw_co2_quantiles} gives a summary of the office rooms
which also had feedback on the temperature in the period of the data. \\
We see that there is almost the same amount of observations for each room besides Room 54, which
has about 1000 more samples. Room 15 has the highest amount of feedback
with 38 valid samples, followed by Room 19, which has 35 valid feedback samples.
We see that the minimum $\text{CO}_2$ ppm values vary a lot and all are below the outdoor minimum value of
around 420 ppm in 2023 \parencite{icos2023ghg}. This is a clear indicator that the sensors need to be calibrated.
We also see that all the rooms have a max value of 5651 ppm, which either indicates that all $\text{CO}_2$ ppm levels have been over this threshold
at some point or that there has been a calibration or error in the sensors that made them go up to the maximum threshold. \\
We also see that the mean of all the rooms sits higher than the median,
which tells us that the data is right-skewed. If we hypothesise that the room is
empty more than 50\% of the time (a working day is 8 hours), then this confirms the theory. \\
\\
We now want to know how much time there is between the observations of the sensors in our office rooms, such
that we can choose a reasonable time step to aggregate the data so that the data is discretised.\\

\input{Tables/data/raw_waiting_times}

In \cref{tab:raw_waiting_times}, we now see a summary table of the
waiting time between each $\text{CO}_2$ reading for the sensors. We notice that Room 54 has about half
the waiting time of the other rooms, and this causes the higher amount of samples.\\
We see that the Q1 to Q3 sit close together, so at least 50\% of the time between each observation
for all rooms is around 10 minutes. We notice that the mean sits close to the median in general,
which indicates that the data is only a little right-skewed, probably caused by a few extreme
waiting times in the rooms. We see that the maximum waiting time for a room is around
1.5 to 2 hours. We should therefore inspect the amount of extreme waiting times and when they happen. \\
\\
Our goal is to discretise the data, but not make it granular enough that we will have to predict too many steps into the future.
We remember that the goal of the models is to be able to provide information about the state of a room 3 to 4 hours into the future. \\
We therefore resample the data into 30 minute bins. This makes it such that all bins contain at least 1 observation,
but most bins will contain around 2 to 3 observations. This gives the nice advantage that an irregular observation will be smoothed out. \\
\\
We also need to look at the feedback counts.
As we saw in \cref{tab:raw_waiting_times}, there was at least 1 waiting time too high for the 30 minute interval. \\

\section{Descriptive Analytics}
Given that our data now has been calibrated as in \cref{eq:calibration_anchor}, we now perform some
descriptive analytics on the data in Room 15 to better understand the properties of our data. \\
The first thing we are interested in is whether our data contains autocorrelation.

\input{Plots/data/room_015_calibrated_acf_pacf.tex}
We clearly see in our plot that there is a 24 hour cycle, where the day after is the second most
correlated cycle. We actually see that after 7 days (336 bins), the correlation rises again, and above the day
after the observations. This indicates that there is a high correlation between 7 days, which also makes sense,
as we then can assume that people have the same behaviour on any given Monday.\\
For the partial autocorrelation plot, we see that after 3 bins, there is no more correlation
between the previous observations. We also see that the second bin is negative, which contradicts
the intuition that a previous observation impacts a current observation negatively, but this can also be
understood as the previous observation just carrying less effect into the current observation.
\bigskip

If we look at the whole data series and mark the off days in \cref{fig:room_015_off_days},
 which are holidays and weekends, we see a clear drop in the $\text{CO}_2$ series on off days, which clearly indicates that
 off days probably do not contain any people in the room.
\input{Plots/data/room_015_off_days.tex}
In \cref{fig:room_015_diurnal_off_days}, we see a clear difference in the pattern of working days and off days, which further
motivates denoting off days and working days. We also see that there is a difference in the $\text{CO}_2$ values when separating
the two types of days over the hours of a day. We see a sudden rise at around 8 and a decline from around 17. This motivates that the working hours
of the offices are 8-17, and that there are people present in those hours.
\input{Plots/data/room_015_diurnal_off_days.tex}

We then check if this is a repeatable behaviour when split out across working days in \cref{fig:room_015_weekly_working_hours}, and
that the general pattern is the same across them. This motivates a covariate denoting the working hours and
also the weekday. There does not seem to be a weekly pattern, such as no people being at the office on Tuesdays.

\section{Summary}
In this chapter, the collection method of the data was described, and the calibration technique was presented in
\cref{eq:calibration_anchor}. We have shown strong autocorrelation in the data with a cyclic pattern that peaks every 24 hours. \\
