\chapter{Theory}
In this chapter, a ODE of the physical model of the $\text{CO}_2$ dynamics will be presented and
how the process is descibed as a stochastic differential equation (SDE), where noise has been added to the system. \\
Given the presented SDE, it will be shown that the process can be desbribed as 
a discrete-time AR(1) process, where the production rate of the occupants in the room is modelled as a function of the occupancy state of the room. \\
Hereafter, the generel theory of the hidden markov models (HMM) will be presented along
with the inference algorithms used in this project. 


\section{Modelling $\text{CO}_2$ dynamics}
This subsection present the underlying physical model of the $\text{CO}_2$ dynamics of a room 
and show that it can be described as a discrete-time AR(1) process and how the production rate of the occupants in the room 
can be modelled as a function of the occupancy state of the room. 

\subsection{The underlying physical model \& assumptions} %Different name for this subsection
Due to $\text{CO}_2$ does not decay by itself, 
the perfect mixtured mass balance model of a room (\cite{batterman2017}; \cite{wang1999})
is given as a first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) as
\begin{equation}
    V \frac{d C_{\text{indoor}}(t)}{dt} = G(t) + Q \left( C_{\text{outdoor}}(t) - C_{\text{indoor}}(t) \right),
    \label{eq:mass-balance}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the room volume (m$^3$), $C_{\text{indoor}}$ and $C_{\text{outdoor}}$ are the
indoor and outdoor $\text{CO}_2$ concentrations (mg$\cdot$m$^{-3}$), $G(t)$ is the occupant
production rate (mg$\cdot$h$^{-1}$) and $Q$ is the outdoor air flow rate (m$^3\cdot$h$^{-1}$).
\bigskip

Dividing by $V$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d C_{\text{indoor}}(t)}{dt} = \frac{G(t)}{V} - \frac{Q}{V} \left( C_{\text{indoor}}(t) - C_{\text{outdoor}}(t) \right).
\end{equation}
Assuming $C_{\text{outdoor}}$ is constant, the excess concentration
$\tilde{C}(t) = C_{\text{indoor}}(t) - C_{\text{outdoor}}$ satisfies
$d\tilde{C}/dt = dC_{\text{indoor}}/dt$, so
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d \tilde{C}(t)}{dt} = \frac{G(t)}{V} - \frac{Q}{V} \tilde{C}(t).
\end{equation}
The sensors report ppm, related by $C\,[\text{ppm}] = C\,[\text{mg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}] \cdot V_m / M_{\text{CO}_2}$,
with $M_{\text{CO}_2} = 44.01$ g$\cdot$mol$^{-1}$ and $V_m \approx 24.45$ L$\cdot$mol$^{-1}$
(25$^\circ$C, 1 atm). Multiplying by the constant $V_m / M_{\text{CO}_2}$ gives the model in ppm,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d \tilde{C}(t)}{dt} = P(t) - \alpha \tilde{C}(t),
    \qquad
    P(t) = \frac{G(t)\, V_m}{M_{\text{CO}_2}\, V},
    \qquad
    \alpha = \frac{Q}{V},
    \label{eq:ODE}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{C}(t)$ is now the excess $\text{CO}_2$ concentration (ppm), $P(t)$ is the
production rate (ppm$\cdot$h$^{-1}$) and $\alpha$ is the air change rate (h$^{-1}$),
i.e.\ the fraction of excess $\text{CO}_2$ ventilated out per hour.
\bigskip

The assumptions in \ref{eq:mass-balance}, and therefore in \ref{eq:ODE}, are that
\begin{itemize}
    \item The $\text{CO}_2$ in the room is perfectly mixed, meaning that the $\text{CO}_2$ is evenly distributed in the room. This ensures that the sensors are not biased by location. 
    \item There is no $\text{CO}_2$ sinks in the room, meaning that the $\text{CO}_2$ is not absorbed by any materials in the room and is only ventilated out of the room. 
\end{itemize}
However, it's important to note that the model in \ref{eq:ODE} is a 
simplification of the real-world $\text{CO}_2$ dynamics of a room, and that 
there are many factors that can affect the $\text{CO}_2$ levels in a room, 
such as the number of occupants, the activity level of the occupants, 
the ventilation system, and the outdoor $\text{CO}_2$ levels. \\
\bigskip
To be able to model the production rate of the occupants in the room, 
we will assume that the production rate $P(t)$ is a stepwise function. 
Let $S(t) = k \in \mathbb{N}$ be the number of occupants of the room
at time $t$, then we can model the production rate as
\begin{equation}
    P(t) = \beta_{S(t)},
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{S(t)}$ is the production rate of the occupants in the room,
\bigskip


One of the main limitations of the model is that it does not assume noise in the system, which is invenatiable as we use sensors. \\
Therefore, the next step to make the model more realistics is to add noise to the system in form of a stochastic differential equation (SDE), which will be presented in the next subsection.

\subsection{The ODE as a diffusion Process (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck)}
As previous described, the model in \ref{eq:ODE} desvibe the dynamics of the $\text{CO}_2$ in a room, 
but it does not take into account the noise in the system. \\
By adding noise to the system in form of a linear diffusion tern with constant diffusion coefficient $\sigma$, 
we can write the model as a stochastic differential equation (SDE) as
\begin{equation}
    d \tilde{C(t)} = (P(t) - \alpha \tilde{C(t)}) d t + \sigma d W(t),
    \label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}
where $W(t)$ is a standard Brownian motion. \\
\ref{eq:sde} is regornized as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, 
which is a mean-reverting process, where the process tends to oscillate 
around some equilibrium state (chap. 1.13.1, \cite{iacus2008}) \\
\bigskip

It is important to stress that the noise term in \ref{eq:sde} was 
assumed to be a linear diffusion term with constant diffusion coefficient $\sigma$,
as this is the simpelst noise to add to the system. However, one could also add
a non-linear difussion term, where the diffusion coefficient is a function of the state of the system. 
This is however out of the scope of this project, as this would require more
in depth knowledge of SDEs and stochastic calculus, which is not the focus of this project. \\
\\
Next we show that, sampled at a fixed time step, the SDE in \ref{eq:sde} is a discrete-time AR(1) process.

\subsection{Properties of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process}
Since \ref{eq:sde} is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, its solution is known in closed form
\parencite[Sec.~1.13.1]{iacus2008}. Sampling it with a constant time step $\Delta t$ and holding
$P$ constant over the step gives the recursion
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{C}_{t+\Delta t} = e^{-\alpha \Delta t}\, \tilde{C}_t
        + \mu \left(1 - e^{-\alpha \Delta t}\right) + e_{t+\Delta t},
    \qquad \mu = \frac{P}{\alpha},
    \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the equilibrium level the process reverts to, and the noise terms
\begin{equation}
    e_{t+\Delta t} = \sigma \int_t^{t+\Delta t} e^{-\alpha (t+\Delta t-u)} \, \mathrm{d}W_u
    \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}\left(1 - e^{-2\alpha \Delta t}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
have mean zero, constant variance and are uncorrelated over time.
\ref{eq:ar1} is therefore an AR(1) process with autoregressive coefficient
$\phi = e^{-\alpha \Delta t}$. The full derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:ar1-proof}.\\
\bigskip
As $\alpha > 0$, from (chapter 1.13.1 \cite{iacus2008}) it is known, that the process is ergodic the distribution of $\tilde{C}_t$ is 
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{C}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{P}{\alpha},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}\right),
\end{equation}
which is the stationary distribution of the process. \\

\subsection{HMM Models}
\subsubsection{Generel Theory}
%% Write some generel theory on HMM
Hidden markov models are markov chains where the underlying states are hidden and only a 
random variable that is dependent on the underlying state is observed. \\
This can be described as a markov chain with a set of hidden states $S = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_N\}$ 
and a set of observations $O = \{o_1, o_2, ..., o_M\}$. 
The transition between the hidden states is governed by a transition matrix $\Gamma$ 
and the observations are generated by an emission matrix $B$. 
The initial state distribution is given by $\pi$. 
The HMM stucture can then debsrib the emissions as 
\begin{equation}
    P(O_t | S_t) = B_{S_t, O_t},
\end{equation}
where $B_{S_t, O_t}$ is the probability of observing $O_t$ given the hidden state $S_t$. 
The transition between the hidden states is given by
\begin{equation}
    P(S_t | S_{t-1}) = \Gamma_{S_{t-1}, S_t},
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{S_{t-1}, S_t}$ is the probability of transitioning from hidden state $S_{t-1}$ to hidden state $S_t$. 
The initial state distribution is given by
\begin{equation}
    P(S_1) = \pi_{S_1},
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{S_1}$ is the probability of starting in hidden state $S_1$.
\subsubsection{HMM(1)-Model}
The ordinary HMM is the simplest model implemented in this project and the one most
commonly used in the literature. The transition probabilities are constant and do not
depend on time, the observations or any covariates, and neither does the emission density.
For this model, we want to estimate the parameters $\theta = \{\Gamma, \mu, \sigma\}$, where
$\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the transition matrix, $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the
mean vector of the Gaussian emission density and $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the standard
deviation vector of the Gaussian emission density. Because the Markov chain is finite,
discrete and homogeneous, a unique strictly positive stationary distribution $\delta$ exists
(chapter 1.3.2, \cite{zucchini2016}) and is given by
\begin{equation}
    \delta = \mathbf{1}^{T} \left( I - \Gamma + E \right)^{-1},
    \label{eq:stationary}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is an $n \times n$ matrix of ones.

To handle the natural bounds on the standard deviations and the transition probabilities,
the parameters are reparameterised. The standard deviations are given by
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_i = \exp(\eta_i).
    \label{eq:sigma-reparam}
\end{equation}
For the transition matrix, the $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal entries are parameterised. The diagonal
is used as the reference category with log-odds fixed to zero, such that the logits form a
matrix of the form
\begin{equation}
    \xi =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -          & \xi_{12} & \cdots & \xi_{1n} \\
        \xi_{21}   & -        & \cdots & \xi_{2n} \\
        \vdots     & \vdots   & \ddots & \vdots   \\
        \xi_{n1}   & \xi_{n2} & \cdots & -
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:xi-matrix}
\end{equation}
Concretely, the unconstrained parameters are stored as a matrix
$\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n-1)}$ that holds the off-diagonal logits. The transition
matrix is then obtained by exponentiating these logits in the off-diagonal positions of an
identity matrix (so the diagonal is $\exp(0) = 1$) and row-normalising:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{ij} =
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{\exp(\xi_{ij})}{1 + \sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\xi_{ik})}, & j \neq i, \\[2ex]
        \dfrac{1}{1 + \sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\xi_{ik})}, & j = i.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:gamma-reparam}
\end{equation}

The $\mu$ parameters are not bounded, but to avoid label switching between the Gaussian
emission distributions, the means are ordered as $\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \dots < \mu_n$ by the
reparameterisation
\begin{equation}
    \mu_1 = \zeta_1, \qquad
    \mu_i = \zeta_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{i} \exp(\zeta_k), \quad i = 2, \dots, n,
    \label{eq:mu-reparam}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. The unconstrained parameters $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$,
$\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (n-1)}$ and $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ are thus the quantities
that are optimised during the fitting process.
\subsubsection{HMM($\beta, 1$)-model} 
In this model, we let the transition probabilities depend on covariates which can include
time-of-day, weekday, type of day and etc. The motivation for this is the assumption, 
that the occupancy state of the room is dependent 
on the time-of-day and weekday, as the room is used for work.  \\

Let $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ be a vector of $p$ covariates at time $t$. 
The transition logits is then defined as
\begin{equation}
    \xi_{ij}(t) = \beta_{ij}^{T} x_t + \tilde{\xi}_{ij}, \qquad i \neq j,
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ is a vector of regression 
coefficients for the transition from state $i$ to state $j$ and 
$\tilde{\xi}_{ij}$ is the intercept term. 
The transition matrix is then obtained as in \ref{eq:gamma-reparam} and we now need denote
the transition matrix as $\Gamma^t_{i,j} = P(S_{t} = j|S_{t-1} = i)$. 


\subsubsection{AR(1)-HMM(1)}
The autoregressive HMM (AR(1)-HMM(1)) extends the ordinary HMM by letting the emission
distribution depend on the previous observation. Conditional on being in state $i$ at time
$t$, the observation is modelled as
\begin{equation}
    Y_t \mid X_t = i,\, Y_{t-1} = y_{t-1} \sim
    \mathcal{N}\!\left( \mu_i + \phi_i \left( y_{t-1} - \mu_i \right),\, \sigma_i^2 \right).
    \label{eq:ar-emission}
\end{equation}
Each state therefore has its own AR(1) coefficient $\phi_i \in (-1, 1)$ in addition to a
mean and a standard deviation. This may be enough to capture the temporal correlation of the
$\text{CO}_2$ signal that the ordinary HMM leaves in the pseudo-residuals. The transition
matrix $\Gamma$, the means $\mu$ and the standard deviations $\sigma$ are parameterised
exactly as in the HMM(1)-model, and the AR coefficients are reparameterised as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_i = 2\, s(\psi_i) - 1, \qquad s(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
    \label{eq:phi-reparam}
\end{equation}
where $s(\cdot)$ is the logistic sigmoid, to enforce the stationarity constraint
$|\phi_i| < 1$. The unconstrained parameter $\psi_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the one passed to the
optimiser, with the inverse mapping
\begin{equation}
    \psi_i = \operatorname{logit}\!\left( \frac{\phi_i + 1}{2} \right).
\end{equation}
\subsubsection{AR(1)-HMM($\beta, 1$)} 
This model is the combination of the AR(1)-HMM(1) and the HMM($\beta, 1$)-model, 
where the transition probabilities depend on covariates as in 
and the emission distribution is autoregressive.

\subsection{Likelihood}
Several methods can be used to estimate the parameters of a HMM, where at 
some of the more common methods are the Baum-Welch algorithm [\textcolor{red}{MISSING REFERENCE}].\\ 
In this project, we will however use the method of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) 
to estimate the parameters of the HMM with the forward-filtering algorithm [\textcolor{red}{MISSING REFERENCE}]. \\

The forward algorithm implemented is called recursive filtering
\parencite{moller2024}, as it constructs the current state distribution
$p(X_t \mid Y_{1:t})$ recursively from the previous state distribution
$p(X_t \mid Y_{1:t-1})$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Recursive Filtering Forward Algorithm}
\label{alg:forward}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Observations $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$, covariates
    $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$, initial state distribution
    $u_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times N}$, HMM parameters $\theta$
\Ensure Predicted distributions $\{u_t\}$, filtered distributions
    $\{u_{t|t}\}$, step likelihoods $\{f_t\}$
\For{$t = 1, \dots, T$}
    \State $\Gamma_t \gets \textsc{TransitionMatrix}(\theta, t, y_t, x_t)$
        \Comment{$N \times N$ transition matrix}
    \State $u_{t|t-1} \gets u_{t-1|t-1} \cdot \Gamma_t$
        \Comment{Predicted state distribution, $1 \times N$}
    \State $g_t \gets \textsc{Density}(\theta, t, y_t, x_t)$
        \Comment{Emission densities for all states, $1 \times N$}
    \State $f_t \gets \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ u_t \odot g_t \right]_i$
        \Comment{Marginal likelihood at time $t$}
    \State $f_t \gets \max(f_t, 10^{-10})$
        \Comment{Clip to avoid division by zero}
    \State $u_{t|t} \gets \dfrac{u_t \odot g_t}{f_t}$
        \Comment{Filtered state distribution (normalized)}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\{u_t\}$, $\{u_{t|t}\}$, $\{f_t\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication of vectors.
The log-likelihood is obtained by summing the log of the step likelihoods:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log f_t .
\end{equation}
Each likelihood step $f_t$ is the sum of the predicted state distribution $u_t$ multiplied by the emission densities $g_t$ for all states, 
which gives the marginal likelihood of observing $y_t$ given the previous observations $y_{1:t-1}$.

\subsection{Backward Sampling} 
When sampling a path from a hidden markov model, we can use the method 
defined in (chapter. 7.2.1, \cite{zucchini2016}) and we will call it backward sampling. \\
We want to sample a path of the hidden states $S_{1:T}$ given the observations 
$O_{1:T}$ and the model parameters $\theta$. This method is very useful, as it 
does not depend on the inital state distribution $\pi$, which is often unknown. \\
Let $u_{t|t} = P(S_t | O_{1:t}, \theta)$ be the filtered state distribution. 
We can then sample the last state $S_T$ from the filtered distribution $u_{T|T}$. 
We can then sample the previous state $S_{T-1}$ from the condition distribution

\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    P(S_{t-1}=j | S_t = i, O_{1:t}, \theta) &= 
    \frac{P(S_{t-1}=j, S_t=i | O_{1:t}, \theta )}{P(S_t=i | O_{1:t}, \theta)} \\
    &=\frac{P(S_{t} = i|S_{t-1} = j, \theta) P(S_{t-1} = j | O_{1:t}, \theta)}{P(S_t=i | O_{1:t}, \theta)}\\
    &=\frac{\Gamma^t_{ji} u_{t-1|t-1}(j)}{u_{t|t}(i)}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\paragraph{Corrected derivation.}
Let $\Gamma^{(t)}_{ji} = P(S_t = i \mid S_{t-1} = j, \theta)$ denote the transition
from time $t-1$ to time $t$. We sample the last state $S_T$ from the filtered
distribution $u_{T|T}$, and then recursively, for $t = T, T-1, \dots, 2$, sample
$S_{t-1}$ given the already sampled $S_t = i$ from the conditional distribution
$P(S_{t-1} = j \mid S_t = i, O_{1:T}, \theta)$. By the Markov property, the future
observations $O_{t:T}$ carry no further information about $S_{t-1}$ once $S_t$ is
known, so
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    P(S_{t-1}=j \mid S_t = i, O_{1:T}, \theta)
    &= P(S_{t-1}=j \mid S_t = i, O_{1:t-1}, \theta) \\
    &= \frac{P(S_{t-1}=j, S_t=i \mid O_{1:t-1}, \theta)}{P(S_t=i \mid O_{1:t-1}, \theta)} \\
    &= \frac{P(S_t = i \mid S_{t-1} = j, \theta)\, P(S_{t-1} = j \mid O_{1:t-1}, \theta)}
            {\sum_{k} P(S_t = i \mid S_{t-1} = k, \theta)\, P(S_{t-1} = k \mid O_{1:t-1}, \theta)} \\
    &= \frac{\Gamma^{(t)}_{ji}\, u_{t-1|t-1}(j)}{\sum_{k} \Gamma^{(t)}_{ki}\, u_{t-1|t-1}(k)}
     = \frac{\Gamma^{(t)}_{ji}\, u_{t-1|t-1}(j)}{u_{t|t-1}(i)},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $u_{t|t-1}(i) = P(S_t = i \mid O_{1:t-1}, \theta)$ is the one-step-ahead
predictive state distribution. The method therefore only requires the filtered
distributions from the forward pass and the transition matrices. Note that it
still depends on the initial distribution $\pi$ through the forward recursion,
although this influence decays for large $t$.

\subsection{Model Diagnostics}
\subsubsection{Goodness-of-fit}
For asessing the goodnes of fit, we will use the pseudo-residuals 
defined in (chapter 7.3, \cite{zucchini2016}). The pseudo-residuals are defined as
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \Phi^{-1}\left(F_{t|t-1}(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the inverse of the standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF)
and $F_{t|t-1}(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})$ is the one-step-ahead predictive CDF of the observation $y_t$ given
the previous observations $y_{1:t-1}$. The pseudo-residuals are expected to be
independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) standard normal if the model is correctly specified. 
Therefore, we can use the pseudo-residuals to assess 
the goodness-of-fit of the model by checking 
if they are normally distributed and uncorrelated. 

\subsubsection{Test Statistics for Comparison}
For comparing the goodnes-of-fit of different models, 
we will use the Akaike information criterion (AIC), 
the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the Likelihood ratio test (LRT).
Let $\hat{L}$ denote the maximised likelihood of a model, $k$ the number of
estimated parameters and $T$ the number of observations.

The AIC \parencite{wiki_aic} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{AIC} = 2k - 2\ln \hat{L},
\end{equation}
and the BIC \parencite{wiki_bic} as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BIC} = k \ln T - 2\ln \hat{L}.
\end{equation}
Both criteria reward a high likelihood and penalise the number of parameters,
and the model with the lowest value is preferred. Since $\ln T > 2$ for $T > 7$,
the BIC penalises additional parameters harder than the AIC and therefore
tends to favour simpler models.

The LRT \parencite{wiki_lrt} compares two nested models, where the null model
with parameter space $\Theta_0$ is a restricted version of the full model with
parameter space $\Theta$. The test statistic is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{LR} = -2\left[\ell(\hat{\theta}_0) - \ell(\hat{\theta})\right],
\end{equation}
where $\ell(\hat{\theta}_0)$ and $\ell(\hat{\theta})$ are the maximised
log-likelihoods under the null and the full model, respectively. By Wilks' theorem,
$\lambda_{LR}$ is asymptotically $\chi^2$-distributed with degrees of freedom
equal to $\dim\Theta - \dim\Theta_0$, provided the null hypothesis lies in the
interior of the parameter space. Note that this condition does not hold when
comparing HMMs with different numbers of states, as the smaller model corresponds
to parameters on the boundary of the larger model's parameter space
(e.g.\ a transition probability equal to zero). The LRT is therefore only used
for comparing nested models with the same number of states.
